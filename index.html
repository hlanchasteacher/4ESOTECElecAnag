Job 01The Flickering Lights

📋 Job ticket: "The workshop lights flicker every time we turn on the soldering iron. Nobody knows why. — Vega"

Before you can fix anything, you need to speak the language of electricity.

Basic electrical quantities

Every circuit is described by three basic quantities, plus power.

QuantitySymbolUnit (SI)Instrument
Voltage / potential differenceVVolt (V)Voltmeter (in parallel)
Current intensityIAmpere (A)Ammeter (in series)
ResistanceROhm (Ω)Ohmmeter (circuit disconnected)
Electrical powerPWatt (W)Wattmeter

Power is energy per unit of time.

P(W) = V(V) × I(A)

Ohm's Law

The current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the voltage across it and inversely proportional to its resistance.

I = V / R

Series, parallel, and mixed circuits

Series circuit

  • Equivalent resistance: R_t = R₁ + R₂ + … + R_n
  • Current: the same through every element (I_t = I₁ = I₂ = …)
  • Voltage: splits across elements. It acts as a voltage divider.
  • Power: generated equals consumed.

Parallel circuit

  • Equivalent resistance: 1/R_t = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …
  • Voltage: the same across every element.
  • Current: splits. It acts as a current divider.

Mixed circuit

Combines series and parallel branches. Solve it in stages: first the parallel blocks, then the resulting series.

Job 02The Silent Guitar Pedal

📋 Job ticket: "A customer brought in a vintage fuzz pedal. The LED doesn't light up, and the output is way too loud. I suspect the resistors. — Vega"

Resistors oppose the flow of current. In electronics they do two key jobs: divide voltage and limit current.

Voltage divider

Two or more resistors in series with a power source. The voltage across each resistor is a fraction of the supply.

V_AB = V · R₁ / (R₁ + R₂)

It's used to feed any component with exactly the voltage it needs.

Current limiter

A resistor in series with another component (for example, an LED) to reduce the current flowing through it.

R = (V − V_LED) / I
Watch out! An LED must always be connected in series with a current-limiting resistor. Otherwise it will burn out.
Good to know: commercial resistors follow standard series (E6, E12, E24…). Series E6 has six values: 1.0 · 1.5 · 2.2 · 3.3 · 4.7 · 6.8. Each value can come in Ω, kΩ or MΩ.

2. What is the purpose of a current-limiting resistor in series with an LED?

3. With V = 9 V, R₁ = 470 Ω and R₂ = 220 Ω, what is the voltage across R₂?

Job 03The Dead Camera Flash

📋 Job ticket: "An old film camera flash won't fire. It needs to store energy and release it in a burst. That's a capacitor's job. — Vega"

A capacitor stores electrical energy when charging and gives it back when discharging.

What capacitors do

  • Timing circuits: charging and discharging take time when there is a resistor in series.
  • Filtering in power supplies (we'll see this in Job 06).

Timing circuits

With a resistor in series, the charge and discharge are slowed down. The characteristic time is:

t_c = t_d = 5 · R · C

where R is in ohms, C is in farads, and the factor 5 means the capacitor is considered fully charged/discharged after five time constants.

Units of capacitance

  • Microfarad (μF): 1 μF = 10⁻⁶ F
  • Nanofarad (nF): 1 nF = 10⁻⁹ F
  • Picofarad (pF): 1 pF = 10⁻¹² F
Safety: large capacitors can stay charged and discharge through your body. Always discharge them through a resistor — never a short circuit.

Job 04The Fried Phone Charger

📋 Job ticket: "A phone charger died. The diodes that convert AC to DC have burned out. Diodes are one-way valves — master them and you control the flow. — Vega"

A diode is a semiconductor that allows current only in the anode → cathode direction (forward bias) and blocks it in the opposite direction (reverse bias).

Rectification

The main use of a diode is rectifying AC: turning it into DC by removing or flipping the negative half of the wave. This is used in power supplies.

  • Half-wave rectifier: one diode. Only lets positive half-cycles through.
  • Bridge rectifier: four diodes. Uses both half-cycles (pulsating DC).

Zener diode

A special diode that also conducts in reverse bias once its Zener voltage (V_Z) is reached. Across it, the voltage stays constant. Used as a voltage regulator.

Key fact: in forward bias, a silicon diode starts conducting at the threshold voltage V_γ ≈ 0.7 V. In reverse bias, exceeding the breakdown voltage destroys a normal diode — but a Zener is designed to work in that region.

Job 05The Mute Radio

📋 Job ticket: "An old AM radio has no sound. The transistor that amplifies the signal is weak. Replace it — but first, understand it. — Vega"

A transistor is a semiconductor with three terminals: emitter (E), base (B), collector (C). It has two main uses: switch and amplifier.

Regions of operation

RegionBehaviorConditions
Cut-offOpen switchV_BE < 0.7 V · I_B = I_C = 0
ActiveAmplifiesV_BE ≥ 0.7 V · I_C = I_B × β
SaturationClosed switchV_BE ≥ 0.7 V · V_CE ≈ 0.2 V · I_C ≤ I_B × β

The gain β (beta) is specific to each transistor, typically between 50 and 300.

Darlington pair

Two transistors in cascade to amplify very weak signals. The total gain is the product:

β = β₁ × β₂

Job 06The Noisy Bench Supply

📋 Job ticket: "The shop's bench power supply hums and gives unstable voltage. Time to understand every stage — transformer, rectifier, filter, regulator. — Vega"

Electronic devices run on DC (12 V or less). The wall socket gives 230 V AC. The power supply turns one into the other.

Stages of a power supply

  1. Transformer: steps down 230 V AC to the required AC voltage.
  2. Bridge rectifier: converts AC into pulsating DC.
  3. Capacitor: filters the ripple, smoothing the wave.
  4. Regulator (Zener + resistor): removes residual ripple and delivers constant voltage.
Chain: 230 V AC → Transformer → Reduced AC → Bridge → Pulsating DC → Capacitor → DC with ripple → Zener → Constant DC.
Tinkercad tip: Tinkercad doesn't have a transformer component. Use the function generator to represent the transformer's reduced AC output (set it to 10 V amplitude, 50 Hz).

Job 07The Blinking Turn Signal

📋 Job ticket: "A customer wants their vintage car's turn signal to blink properly again. The 555 timer is the classic chip for this. Master it — and you'll control time itself. — Vega"

The 555 IC controls how long a device is powered. It has two main modes: monostable (timer) and astable (oscillator).

Key pins

  • Pin 2 (trigger): if V < V_CC/3, output goes HIGH (V_CC).
  • Pin 3 (output): supplies V_CC or 0 V.
  • Pin 6 (threshold): if V > 2·V_CC/3, output goes LOW (0 V).

Monostable mode (timer)

Output stays LOW until the trigger is pulsed. Then it goes HIGH for a time T and returns LOW.

T = 1.1 · R · C

Astable mode (oscillator)

Output swings periodically between HIGH and LOW. No stable state. Used in blinkers, alarms, buzzers…

T₁ = 0.693 · (R₁ + R₂) · C   |   T₂ = 0.693 · R₂ · C

Job 08The Dusk Sensor

📋 Job ticket: "A customer's security light won't turn on at dusk. The op-amp is the brain that compares voltages and decides. Fix it. — Vega"

The 741 op-amp is an IC that amplifies very weak signals. It compares the voltages at its two inputs.

Key pins

  • Pin 2: inverting input (V−)
  • Pin 3: non-inverting input (V+)
  • Pin 6: output (V_s)
  • Pin 4: negative supply (−V_CC)
  • Pin 7: positive supply (+V_CC)

Output voltage:

V_s = A_v · (V+ − V−)

where A_v is the gain (around 100 000). The output is limited by the supply rails: V_s max = ±V_CC.

Comparator application

  • If V+ > V−, then V_s = +V_CC.
  • If V+ < V−, then V_s = −V_CC.

Used, for example, to control room lighting based on ambient light (with an LDR) or temperature (with a thermistor).
